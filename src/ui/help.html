Parameter Space Inspector · manual

Help

The Parameter Space Inspector (PSI) opens a language model and lets you watch it: how it answers, what happened inside while it did, when its abilities appeared during training, what it depends on, what it understood, and whether it knows itself. It is part of The Emergence Machine and tests the site’s ideas on a real network. This page ships with the app and works offline. An AI agent using it to learn about itself should start at For agents: what PSI can and cannot tell you about yourself.

What it is

Two engines run the same model, plus a set of training checkpoints:

  • The answering engine (llama.cpp, a .gguf file: the local model) writes answers and gives the odds of every next piece. It powers Generate (with its roads), Read, Lens on / off and the pieces in Tokens. Fast, any GGUF.
  • The inside-the-model engine (PyTorch, the full-precision weights) reads every layer, neuron and attention head. It powers Neural net, Anatomy, the live parts of the Experiments and the word map in Tokens. It starts on first use and uses the GPU when about 9 GB is free.
  • Training checkpoints (Pythia-410m, 21 saved steps) let you ask the model as it was at a point in its training (Emergence).

Optionally, frontier models (GPT, Gemini, Claude) are reachable through OpenRouter with your own key, for comparison and for Prometheus, the built-in guide.

The strip at the top right reads this computer: three small meters, CPU, RAM and GPU, each an icon with its value and a thin bar that fills with use. Hover (or focus) a meter for its name and details. Only the GPU meter changes colour: amber while a long job (a Storage ladder run, a Grow) holds the card, and red with ⚠ when a model has spilled out of the card into system memory, where it keeps running many times slower without an error. Click the strip for Settings > This computer.

For agents: what PSI can and cannot tell you about yourself

This section is for an AI agent using the Inspector over MCP to learn about its own thinking, and for the person watching it. The same text comes back in psi_status as for_agents.

PSI cannot read your weights or activations. Every inside measurement is of an open model named in conditions: an analogue, not you.

Three routes:

  1. Study the analogue. Run the instruments on the open model (layers, neurons, heads, deletions, training checkpoints). It costs GPU time. What you learn is a mechanism to test on yourself, not a reading of you.
  2. Score your own text under an open model. score_my_text puts the context in the user turn and your text in the assistant turn, then gives the open model’s surprise at each piece of your text. Beside it: the open model’s own greedy answer, and a human-written passage, scored the same way. That is the open model’s surprise, not where you chose or how sure you were.
  3. Sample yourself through OpenRouter and compare. self_consistency and framing_side_by_side ask a frontier model (perhaps you) several times or under several framings. This is behaviour only. It spends the user’s money, under their cap, and each call states its max_usd.

Four findings set the limits:

  • Saying the right thing about yourself is not access to yourself. One model rated red’s associates correctly (AUC 0.91) while its internal state disagreed (rho −0.13).
  • Your top-1 probability already predicts your correctness as well as any probe found here.
  • Part of any self-report score is the prompt. An untrained network scored 0.84 on a self-report task.
  • Nothing that changes a model or spends money runs without the user’s yes. Start with psi_status.

What transfers, and what does not

May transfer (as a hypothesis to test by your behaviour)Does not transfer
Mechanisms common to transformer models: an answer becoming readable in late layers, copying by look-back heads, abilities appearing in a set order in trainingThe numbers: probabilities, margins, AUCs, bits
Failure patterns: surface-plausible distractors, how much evidence moves an answer, sensitivity to framingLayers, neurons, heads and bins: your architecture is not this one

Every result carries an analogue block that says this per tool. It has measured (for example Qwen3-4B-Q8_0.gguf, Q8_0, llama.cpp, stored result, your text scored by … or your sampled output via OpenRouter (behaviour only)), you_measured: false, transfers and does_not_transfer.

Five sentences to keep

  • A direction is not a concept. A vector that separates “red” sentences from others is a readout. The model may not use it, and it may carry other things too.
  • A signature vouches for origin, not ability. A signed seed was made by The Emergence Machine. That says nothing about what it can do.
  • One flip is an anecdote, framing sensitivity is a rate. One answer that changed under one framing proves little. Count changes over many prompts and samples, and give the interval.
  • The logit lens shows when an answer becomes readable at one layer through one readout, not when it was decided.
  • Bounds stay bounds. A piece outside the local engine’s top 20 is off scale: its surprise is “at least” a value, never a reading.

The first ten minutes

  1. Generate: press Generate on the prompt that is there. Brighter pieces are where it was torn; click one to see the roads it did not take; below the die, ask what one choice closed off.
  2. Neural net: press Answer and record, then drag the token slider. Watch the answer form through the layers: for about two thirds of the network it is present but not yet readable.
  3. Anatomy: under “watch it run”, press Record a forward pass, then Play. Click a bright bin to see its real neurons; click a neuron for where it fires in ordinary text.
  4. An experiment: open Emergence and press Compare step 512 and 1,000 to see the model before and after it learned to use context (needs the training checkpoints).
  5. Ask Prometheus (Ctrl+/): “what am I looking at?” Prometheus answers only after you save an OpenRouter key and tick Prometheus, the AI guide in Settings > External models (OpenRouter). Until then the shortcut opens a setup card that says what is missing and links to the place to fix it.

Red text in a prompt box is past what the engine can read. Every box that feeds a model measures your text with that model's own tokenizer against the context the engine actually runs, not the one the model was trained for: Qwen3-4B is trained for 40,960 pieces but the local engine runs it with 8,192 by default, and the model line under the title shows both (“trained for 40,960 · this engine runs 8,192”). The toy reads 128 pieces and the Pythia checkpoints in Emergence 2,048. The part beyond the limit turns red, and a note under the box says how much does not fit. The model never sees the red part: shorten the text, or run the engine with a longer context.

Every view has its own address (#anatomy, #fight…), so refresh, bookmarks and back/forward keep your place. This manual uses the same names: help.html#anatomy opens the Anatomy section.

Mixture-of-experts models (gpt-oss, Qwen3-MoE, Mixtral…) that do not fit beside what is already on the GPU keep the experts of just enough layers on the processor (llama.cpp’s --n-cpu-moe), so nothing spills into shared memory; Settings > Models says how many. The inside engine takes the GPU when the model fits in its free memory (the weights × 1.15 + 1 GB), else the processor, and says why. PSI_DEVICE=cpu|cuda|mps|xpu chooses by hand. Only NVIDIA has been tested on real hardware so far; the others follow each library’s documented interface and were tested with simulated readings.

Remote use

The browser server answers only on this computer (127.0.0.1). It has no login of its own, so to use it from another device put it behind a proxy that authenticates you first (for example a Cloudflare Tunnel with Cloudflare Access, or an SSH tunnel), and add that host name to PSI_ALLOWED_HOSTS. A request to that host is answered only if it carries Cloudflare Access’s Cf-Access-Jwt-Assertion header; with another proxy, forward to 127.0.0.1:4310 (an SSH tunnel does). Never expose port 4310 directly.

The views

The left menu of the app groups them. Each section below says what you can do, what it measures, and what it needs (see also the table). Button and checkbox names are quoted as they appear in the app.

Every result says what produced it. A line under the button row (“run on”, “recorded on”, “read from”) names the model, the engine (local or inside), the framing, how pieces were picked (creativity, seed, greedy) and which numbers are estimated. While steering, a LoRA or a Tipping-point cut is active, a changed chip shows in the header on every view, and results from the changed model are marked. Values the instrument cannot read are shown as off scale, never as a number.

Watch it think

Generate #generate

Needs: the local model.

Ask the local model anything and press Generate (or Ctrl+Enter); +20 tokens carries on from where it stopped. The answer is coloured by how unsure it was at each piece (brighter = more torn; a ▴ marks doubt past the colour scale at 5 bits). The fork map lists every step with the model’s odds for each alternative; click a choice to force that road and re-write from there. A forced step is forced by you, not drawn: the ribbon underlines it dotted, the fork map and the die say so. The die shows the selected step’s die odds: the model’s odds reshaped by creativity. steps, creativity (temperature) and choices shown are adjustable.

The “run on” line above the text says what that text was made with, run by run: the model and any change to it, the framing, the sampler (“creativity, drawn in the app from the top 20”: the app draws from the model’s 20 likeliest choices itself, without a fixed seed, so a rerun differs), why each run ended (end token, step limit, context full, stopped by you, or not reported), and the engine’s speed in tokens/s (“slow for this GPU?” under 5, which is how a spill into system memory looks). show exact prompt opens the text sent to the model, verbatim. The doubt in bits is exact where the choices read held 98% of the odds; otherwise the engine is asked for its top 200 for that step, and “≥” remains where even those did not. The Show its thinking switch appears only for a model whose chat format knows “/no_think”; when it is off, the conditions say “‘/no_think’ appended”. For other models the question is sent as written. If the model, steering or LoRA changes, the text on screen is frozen and marked “made with a previous model/steering”: +20, forks and basins are disabled on it until you Generate afresh (a model with another tokenizer can never continue it). The lens and steering themselves are set in Settings > Thinking in E-levels.

Generate › where the roads go #generate, below the die

Needs: the local model, and text generated above it.

The part of Generate below the die (it was once a view of its own, Paths & attractors; an old #paths link opens it). What one choice closed: click a word in the generated text to pick that step (or press Pick the first real fork when asked), choose an alternative, and press Analyse this fork to see the road taken beside the road not taken. Basins from here: Map the basins samples several roads (roads sampled) from the start of the answer or from where the text ends now, and groups where they settle. Attractors in the text marks loops the answer falls into. Shows how one early choice closes off later ones. Both analyses use the server sampler (llama-server draws the roads itself, unlike Generate’s die); the conditions line shows the settings it reported using, why the roads ended and its speed. They are seeded, so usually repeatable (GPU arithmetic can differ between runs). The escape point is given as the range between two tried settings, or as off scale when it lies outside them; with 16 roads a share is ±12 points (one standard error), so an escape whose shares span 50% is labelled “within noise”. A fork verdict comes with a same-road control: each road’s continuations are split in half, and anything that also differs between two halves of one road is struck through as within noise. Level readings show their votes; with the lens on, the model is told to write E-numbers, so each reading is also given with those mentions left out. A loop’s doubt keeps its “at least” and is shown beside the doubt before the loop.

Neural net #neural

Needs: the inside engine.

The same model seen from inside while it answers. Press Answer and record (choose how many tokens; E-level framing, a system prompt asking it to think in the fifteen levels, is on by default), then scrub the token slider. Each recording is numbered (“recording #2”), and its conditions line says what it was measured on, as the inside engine reported it: the model folder and a short hash of its weights, the precision and device, whether a Tipping-point cut was in force, whether “/no_think” was appended, the framing, and “no untrained baseline here” (nothing in this view is compared with an untrained twin). The inside engine reads an unpacked folder (bf16 safetensors), not the GGUF the File view shows; if their layers, width or vocabulary differ, the line says so.

  • Speed, under the buttons after each recording: tokens/s while recording (the forward pass plus reading out every layer), model alone (the forward pass by itself, keeping every layer’s states and attention), the milliseconds per token for each, the first token (reading the prompt), the prompt read rate, the total, the peak GPU memory and the precision. With an NVIDIA card: peak power (of the card’s limit), average power (and the reading just before), the energy used, the energy per token, and the energy above idle. Power is the whole card’s, read from the driver (NVML): it includes anything else running on that GPU, such as the answering engine. Without an NVIDIA card these are left out. Recording is much slower than Generate: it keeps and reads out every layer, and runs the plain (eager) attention code so the attention weights can be read.
  • What each layer would say (the logit lens): each layer’s top reading, with ticks for its top three. The dashed line marks the layer from which the reading stays the token written, or “decided only at the last layer”; a dot marks an earlier layer where it showed and then gave way. Hover a column, or use the layer slider, for the top three as text.
  • Neurons that fired hardest: the 8 with the largest activation per layer. Once the normal range is built (each neuron’s mean and SD over 800 passages of ordinary text, saved per weights), dots are sized by z-score, so layers are comparable; before that, by raw activation, which is not. One scale for every token of a recording.
  • Attention at the chosen layer, averaged over all heads, over the positions as the model saw them: template tokens grey, the E-level framing dotted, its own earlier words underlined, and a leading “</think>” the model wrote kept greyed (left out of the slider, not out of the context). The summary splits the share that went to the prompt into your question, the framing and the template.
  • Knock-outs: Knock out the 8 at this layer or The 8 at every layer re-asks the recorded prompt with the recorded framing (not what is in the box now), with the tokens before the chosen one as written. switched off = “set to zero” (zero-ablation) or “held at their average” (mean-ablation, needs the normal range). Each result is stamped “token i/N of recording #k” and comes with a random control: 5 random sets of 8 per layer in the same layers, shown as a range. “Nothing changed” or “no different from any 8” is said only when the result lies inside that range.

Read the surface

Read #read

Needs: the local model.

Paste any text (or pick plain text or absorption: a word it has never seen) and press Read it; the model reads it piece by piece and colours it by surprise. Hover a piece for its probability, its surprise in bits, its rank among the model’s guesses, its doubt (entropy, “≥” where it is a lower bound) and surprise − doubt: how much more surprised it was than it expected to be. Pieces outside its top 20 guesses are off scale: drawn hatched, shown as “over N bits”, and left out of the plain average; the headline leads with a bounded average that counts them at their floor (“average surprise ≥ X bits, counting each off-scale piece at its floor”), the plain one in brackets after it. A ▴ marks surprise past the colour scale at 12 bits. A small histogram below shows how the surprise is spread. The text is read with no beginning-of-text token (“no BOS”), so the first piece is not scored, and special tokens are not parsed.

Tokens #tokens

Needs: the local model for the pieces; the inside engine for the rest.

Show the pieces: the pieces the model actually sees, cut by the GGUF’s tokenizer with special tokens not parsed (try the language buttons: the same idea costs more pieces in some languages). Show where they live: the model’s whole dictionary flattened to a map, your pieces on it, and for the selected piece its row of numbers and nearest pieces. It reads the inside engine’s copy of the model: if that engine cuts the text differently from the GGUF tokenizer, it says so. Neighbour cosines come with a typical-pair cosine and the median row length when the engine reports them, and “no reference value” when it does not. Read it and show what each word became: what the model expects after an invented word, after layers 6, 12, 24 and 36, at its first and second mention.

Lens on / off #compare

Needs: the local model; an OpenRouter key for the external models.

The same question asked with and without the E-level lens (a system prompt framing it through the fifteen levels): press Ask both ways, with tokens setting the length. Both answers are greedy (the top choice each step, repeatable). Each column says why it ended; a thinking model that never closed its thinking block within the tokens allowed is marked “answer not reached”. show exact prompt shows both prompts as sent. Tick also ask the external models chosen in Settings, both ways to compare frontier models side by side; choose them in Settings > External models > models to compare against.

Experiments

Each experiment comes from an Emergence Machine essay and opens with its question. The numbered chips under the title jump to each instrument; the menu counts how many experiments you have run. Some instruments run live on the model; others show recorded results from the research, as noted.

Experiment designer #design

Needs: the inside engine; an OpenRouter key for Prometheus to draft plans (the built-in example runs without one).

Test your own idea. Ask Prometheus, for example “does the model take the same path when one question is asked in different languages?”. It drafts a plan: the prompts, a baseline that tells a real effect from chance, and what to measure. The plan appears here first. Read it, edit any prompt (a translation you would word differently, say), then press Run it, and watch Prometheus do it, or Decline and say what to change. Prometheus then runs the plan in front of you, the way you would: in its panel each step is a card, and its hand opens Neural net, sets the length and framing, types each prompt, presses Answer and record and points at what came out the first time. When every prompt is recorded it comes back here and the report is drawn from those recordings. Press Stop in its panel to end early; what was recorded is kept. Run in the background makes the same recordings without the tour, faster, with a progress bar. Load the example runs the languages experiment without Prometheus: “Why is the sky blue?” and, as the baseline, “How do bees make honey?”, each in English, Spanish, Hindi, Chinese, Arabic and Russian.

Each prompt is recorded in the inside model as Neural net records it (greedy). The app computes every number itself and draws:

  • Neuron overlap by layer: for each kind of pair (same question in another language; another question in the same language; neither), how many of the hardest-firing neurons the two answers share (the Jaccard index: shared neurons divided by all neurons in either). The band runs from the least to the most similar pair. A shared path for the meaning sits above both baselines.
  • Every pair: the overlap of each pair of prompts in the middle third of the layers.
  • What all of them share: neurons that fired for every language of one question, beside groups of the same size where the question changes.
  • Which language each layer thinks in: the logit lens for the first answer token, coloured by script. Middle layers that read in another script than the prompt’s suggest the model passes through another language on the way; the view names the script read most often. For Qwen3-4B it is Chinese: an English and a Hindi “why is the sky blue?” both read 回答 (“answer”) and 这个问题 (“this question”) in the middle layers. Brown cells are pieces of a character the tokenizer split. Latin-script languages cannot be told from English this way, and the view says so.
  • The answers, shaded by how sure the model was; Open in Neural net loads any of them into that view, where you can scrub the tokens and knock neurons out.

A plan can also use Read instead of Neural net: each prompt is read by the local model and its surprise is measured in bits, piece by piece. With two versions of each sentence (true and false, say, differing in one word) the report pairs them up: how many more bits the false one cost, sentence by sentence. The tour then runs in Read: it types each sentence and presses Read it.

Every instrument. A plan names one instrument and runs it on every prompt: Neural net (recordings: neuron overlap, the logit lens), Read (surprise in bits), Generate (the greedy answer; how alike the answers are), next-token odds (how torn it is about the next piece), Tokens (how many pieces), Word map (nearest words in its map of meaning), Connectors (position on the connection axis), Fight class (the probe’s reading), Sensing surface (structural surprise), When was it learned? (the Pythia training step; about 5 minutes per prompt), Understanding tests (good against bad continuation, and the layer it is readable from), knock-outs (how much of the first answer word lives in the hardest-firing neurons, beside random sets) and activation patching (where the answer is decided; the clean and corrupt prompt must split into the same number of pieces). Every number comes from the app’s own tool call for that instrument, the same one its view and agents use. The tour opens the instrument’s view and types each prompt into its own boxes; it presses the view’s button where that is quick and changes nothing (where it does not, its panel says why). A prompt an instrument refuses is shown as not measured, with the instrument’s reason, and the rest of the experiment goes on. Not in the designer: Tipping-point cuts (they change the model until restored), external frontier models (they spend money), and growing or signing seeds (they write files).

Writing an experiment. Type the idea in Describe your experiment and press Send to Prometheus (Ctrl+Enter): it drafts the plan, which still waits for your Run. While a tour runs, Prometheus’s panel has three controls beside Stop: Slower (the hand moves and types more slowly, with a beat after each step), Pause / Resume (after the current step), and Run the rest in the background (the tour stops moving through the views; the designer finishes the remaining prompts without it, with a progress bar). Stop takes effect after the step in progress.

Tipping point in an experiment. A plan with the Tipping-point instrument gives each prompt a cut: none, neurons:0.2, heads:0.1, both:0.3 (a share up to 0.9, optionally :most or :least by activity on that prompt) or concept:cricket. Before it starts it refuses if a cut of yours is already in force (it never undoes your cut), then records the intact model’s answers. Emergence #emergence · essay: Where Understanding Begins

Needs: the inside engine; the training checkpoints for 1 and 2.

1 ask the model at a training step: move the slider and Ask at this step, or Compare step 512 and 1,000. 2 when did it learn this: your test scored at every checkpoint (Find the step; tick all checkpoints for the full run). 3 the heads that look back in the loaded model: Scan all heads, then knock out a number of the strongest induction heads or random heads (control) and see what dies with them.

When did it learn this. “Learned” means the margin rose above the noise (0.5 nats) and stayed above it at every later step scored; the verdict names the bracket (between step A and step B) and how many checkpoints were scored. Inside ±0.5 nats it says “within noise”. When the two answers share their first piece they are compared whole; when they are the same pieces to the tokenizer the checkpoint says “answers indistinguishable at first piece”, not “not learned”. If unscored checkpoints lie inside the bracket, Score every checkpoint between narrows it.

Copying, everywhere in the app (here, the head scan, the knock-outs and the Tipping-point scorecard): 40 random tokens (ids 100–30,000) shown twice, 3 trials, seed 0; nats saved = loss on the first copy minus loss on the second. A head “looks back” when its induction attention is above 0.3. The heat map shows the induction score or, from heat map shows, the previous-token score. Timeline #timeline

Needs: nothing for the chart (recorded results). Placing a model or grafting: Python with torch and transformers, and the model as a Hugging Face folder in your model listing (Settings > Models > Scan). Small models run on the CPU; 7 B models need the GPU.

1 · What emerged when. Nineteen capabilities, from beating a frequency table to handling “not”, and the point of training at which each appeared in five Pythia sizes (70m to 2.8B), measured at their saved checkpoints. The onset is the first checkpoint where the test passes and keeps passing at every later checkpoint; because checkpoints are sparse, it lies somewhere between the checkpoint before and that one, and the chart draws that interval. Click a row for its card: what the test is, its pass line, the onset in each size, and the mirage check (Schaeffer et al. 2023): where a continuous measure stands behind a pass/fail one, whether it also jumps in the onset interval (sudden) or rose steadily and only the cut-off made the onset look sudden. A shaded band marks three or more emergences in one interval. 2 · Place a model on it. Runs the same tests (the research scripts themselves, imported) on any model folder: an OLMo or Pythia checkpoint, a seed you trained, a finished model. It shows which capabilities the model has, the recorded checkpoint it most resembles, and, at that checkpoint, what emerged next. A test whose right and wrong answers begin with the same piece in this model’s vocabulary is not decidable (the tests compare first pieces), never a failure. The diamonds on the chart are placed models. Each result carries a receipt: SHA-256 of every model file, of the code that measured, and of the numbers, signed if you chose a key in Commons › Profile & keys. Check receipt verifies the signature, that the numbers are unchanged and that the model files still hash the same. Several placed checkpoints of one family can be signed as a series: one Merkle root over their receipts. A signature says which install produced which numbers from which files; it does not say the numbers are right. Measuring again does: a placement is deterministic on the CPU, so a checkpoint placed again lands at distance 0.00 from its recorded self.

3 · Graft lab. Puts a donor model’s layers in place of some of the host’s and measures text (bits per token), copying, facts and agreement, against the gap (the host with those layers removed, nothing put in) and a random graft (the donor’s layers re-initialised). Fit = (gap − graft) / (gap − host): 1 means the graft does the removed layers’ job, 0 no better than the gap, below 0 worse than the gap; when the host is within 0.05 bits (0.5 nats of copying) of the gap there is no scale, and the gain against the host is the number to read. How the layers go in: drop-in (same architecture and attention shape: as they are), same width (another architecture: they run with their own positions), stitched (another width, same vocabulary: an affine map each way, fitted by ridge regression on 8,192 tokens of one half of the held-out texts; its R² on the other half is reported). Align fits the maps even when the widths match. Sweep grafts a window at every depth and draws a heat map; rank donors tries one mid-depth layer from each. Nothing on disk changes. What it has shown so far: layers are not parts. Even the same model’s own layers from step 16,000 do worse than the gap in the finished model at 10 of 12 depths, and every trained layer put into the step-512 model makes it worse than a frequency table, until a fitted map translates between them; then the young model copies for the first time.

4 · What fits here. For any model in the listing: how each way of training it would fit on this GPU and how fast. Every weight with a full-precision optimizer (16 bytes per weight) or an 8-bit one (6 bytes); adapters (LoRA, rank 16 on every projection) with the frozen weights in 16 bits or in 4 bits (QLoRA). The estimate adds what gradient checkpointing keeps per layer, one live layer and the loss over the vocabulary (sequence 1,024, batch 1); speed assumes 40% of the card’s bf16 peak. Measure runs 1 warm-up and 3 training steps on held-out text and records the peak memory and tokens per second, capped at the free memory so a misfit stops with “out of memory” instead of spilling into system RAM. Nothing is saved. The card also lists the models whose layers can be grafted into this one, and how (drop-in, same width, stitched).

Tipping point #lesion · the reverse of training

Needs: the inside engine.

Record the intact answer to a question first (it is done for you otherwise). 1 delete a share of the model: what (neurons + heads, or raw weights of the MLP, attention or all), how (at random, least active / smallest, most active / largest) and share, then Delete and score, or Sweep 0–70%. 2 delete by hand: layers, heads, and how many most-active neurons per layer. 3 delete a concept, such as cricket, keeping a related one (Find its neurons, then Delete it and score). A scorecard after each cut: understanding tests, copying, ordinary text, and retention (how much of its own original answer it still gives).

Reading the scorecard. Understanding is the live battery’s 8 core tests (passed = log-odds > 0, a fixed threshold) with the self-referential test and your own tests shown apart; press the count for every test’s margin beside the intact row’s. Every row is read against the intact row, the 0% cut of the same model, and its colours are relative to it (green: at least 90% of its passes, 75% of its copying, at most +0.5 bits; red: half its passes or fewer, under 25% of its copying, more than +2 bits). Each row states the share actually removed and how it was ranked: “least / most active” ranks neurons by activation on your question and heads by induction score. Negative copying (the second copy got harder) is marked off the scale, not drawn as 0.

The sweep runs 3 seeds per level for a random cut (one for a ranked cut, which is deterministic), draws the min–max band, and gives the tipping point as an interval across seeds. Rows and sweeps are kept in this browser per model; a reply from other weights, or a switch of the inside engine’s model, archives them and clears the intact row.

A cut stays in place until you press Restore the model or cut again; a sweep restores the model when it ends. Neuron and head deletions are masks and cost nothing to undo. Raw-weight deletions are zeroed in place and restored from the weight files on disk (or, for a model with none, from a copy kept in system memory), each tensor checked against its source; this takes a few seconds.

The cut is in the inside engine’s model, which Understanding, Emergence (3), Sensing surface, Fight class, Connectors and Anatomy also read. While it is in place those views show a banner, each live result says “on the changed model”, and the header shows a changed chip. The line under Restore the model says what is cut now. The scorecard reads every row against the intact model once a sweep has scored it (its 0% level); a measurement that could not be made reads “not scored”, never 0.

Save this cut model keeps a cut: a new model folder beside the original, which is never changed (an optional name; otherwise <model>-cut-<kind>-<date>). The weight files are read from disk again and the cut written into them: deleted neurons become zeroed columns of that layer’s down_proj, deleted heads zeroed columns of o_proj (the same outputs as the masks), raw-weight cuts the same zeros; every other value is the original’s own. Config and tokenizer are copied, and cut.json records the cut, the original’s weights hash and the SHA-256 of every file. Each changed tensor is read back before the folder gets its name; a failed save leaves no folder and the cut in force. Then Make a GGUF (8-bit) converts it for the answering engine, and push a saved cut model to Hugging Face below it sends it there (any saved cut folder; a save fills it in) (Settings > Hugging Face) (as Settings > Models does), or load the folder as the inside engine’s model. It needs the model to have been loaded from safetensors files and free space for one more copy. For agents it needs the permission to change models.

Storage ladder #ladder · essay: Abstraction

Needs: nothing for 1 to 6, 8 and 9 (recorded results). Instrument 7 runs on a Hugging Face model folder you choose, best on a GPU.

How a model stores one concept, red, at nine levels (M0–M8): M0 the word’s vector, M1 single neurons, M2 directions, M3 the layout of meanings across languages, M4 the wiring between neurons, M5 combinations built while reading (red light, Chinese and US stock screens), M6 backups, M7 a layout shared with other labs’ models, M8 self-report coherence (said vs held: does what it says about red match what its own state holds). A level counts only where the trained model beats its untrained twin (the same architecture with random weights).

Read M7 with its twins. Untrained models from different labs agree with each other on red’s layout about as much as trained models do (in the stored runs of October 2026, a mean rho of about 0.55 between twins of different labs and 0.58 between trained models). With random weights the layout follows the words the sentences share, so agreement between trained models is not, on its own, a layout they learned. M7’s verdict compares a model with untrained models instead (about 0.1 to 0.3), which trained models clearly beat. Card 6 puts the two comparisons side by side; the numbers there come from the current data.

The data and the analysis

Under every report: the data, every number the experiment recorded as one long table (a row per prompt × measure × layer × token: prompt, the factors, measure, layer, token, piece, neuron, value, note), to sort, filter by measure and search. Excel exports it with the prompts, the plan, the summary and each analysis’s tables as sheets; PowerPoint makes a deck (the question, the design, a slide per chart, Prometheus’s reading, how it was measured); CSV is the long table.

Analysis runs JavaScript over that table in a sandbox: no network, no files, nothing loaded, 20 seconds at most, randomness seeded. It has a statistics kit (intervals, Welch and paired t-tests, permutation tests, bootstrap, effect sizes, correlation, regression, grouping and pivots) and, for Layer vectors experiments, linear probes (cross-validated accuracy against chance), CKA and PCA. Results are text, numbers, tables and charts, kept with the experiment beside the code that made them. Prometheus can write analyses too (analyze_data), and read the history (list_experiments, read_experiment); you see every one, with its code.

Two instruments exist for the analysis: Layer vectors keeps the model’s state after every layer (projected to 256 numbers by a fixed random matrix, which keeps distances and angles) for probes and similarity, and Features records which SAE features fire on each prompt.

Features #features

Needs: the inside engine, and a sparse autoencoder (SAE) for at least one layer of the inside model (train or import one in the view).

A layer’s state is a few thousand numbers, and one neuron rarely means one thing. An SAE rewrites the state as a few active features out of many thousands, each a direction that tends to mean one thing.

  • Which features fire on this text: each piece shaded by its strongest feature; click a piece for its features, or a feature for its card. Unexplained is the share of the state the SAE does not reconstruct on the text. The first piece is left out of the totals (its state is an outlier, the attention sink).
  • The card: its name (yours, Prometheus’s, or the words it promotes until named), how often it fires, its largest activation, the words its direction promotes and suppresses, its strongest examples from the text sample, and steer an answer with it: the plain answer beside one with the feature added after its layer (strength in units of its largest activation; negative removes it).
  • Find a feature: by name, by its examples, by the words it promotes, and by what fires on a phrase.
  • Why this next word?: for each layer with an SAE, the features that pushed the model’s next word up or down most (activation × gradient, a first-order estimate), and how much the SAE leaves unexplained.
  • Add an SAE: train one on a layer (TopK; 4×, 8× or 16× the layer’s size; 200,000 to 1,000,000 pieces of the neuron corpus or your own text sample). It loads only the layers up to the one it reads, stops both engines while it runs and takes a few minutes on a GPU. Or import a downloaded one (EleutherAI sparsify, SAE Lens, Gemma Scope) made for the same model.
  • How good is the SAE? Evaluate puts its reconstruction in place of the layer’s state and measures the model’s next-piece loss on held-out text: loss recovered (against the state replaced by its mean; 100% would be no loss at all) and the loss added in nats per piece (lab SAEs add well under 0.1), with the unexplained share, active features per piece and features that never fired. A new SAE is evaluated when its training ends. Score 10 names asks a judge model (Settings: the OpenRouter judge) to say, from a feature’s name alone, which of ten held-out snippets it fires on (five where it fires most, five where it does not): balanced accuracy, 0.5 being chance. It spends about a cent per ten. Training keeps the last 5% of its text sample back for these checks; an SAE trained on all of it is marked in-sample, which measures fit rather than generalisation.
  • A larger text sample: the neuron corpus is about 250,000 pieces. Get a larger text sample downloads WikiText-103 (Wikipedia’s good and featured articles, CC BY-SA, about 150 MB from Hugging Face) and keeps about 15 million pieces of it; training then streams the layer’s states through a fixed buffer, so a large sample does not need a large memory.
  • Rings: every SAE trained or imported adds a ring to the inside model’s chain, signed by The Emergence Machine as grown seeds are: ring 0 observes the model (by its weights hash, unchanged); each later ring records one SAE (its own hash, the layer, the data with its SHA-256, the training, how well it reconstructs). The view checks every signature and certificate, and Verify online sends the ring records (never the SAE or the model) to emergencemachine.com, which checks them on its own and signs its answer; the view keeps that answer after checking it against the site’s certificate. python src/sae_rings.py verify <SAE folder> checks a folder offline, revocation list included.

Anatomy #anatomy

Needs: the inside engine; an OpenRouter key for the parts written by Prometheus.

The loaded model’s real structure, drawn from its weights.

  • The network: one column per layer (consecutive layers share a column on narrow screens), heads on top, neurons below in bins. Bins adapt to the model and the screen; neuron order inside a layer is arbitrary, so a bin is a down-sampling. Grey links are the strongest bin-to-bin connections, coloured links the strongest single neurons. Click a column for that layer’s weight maps.
  • Watch it run: Record a forward pass (choose tokens to write, optionally with the E-level lens), then Play, pause, scrub, or Jump to the answer (Space, ← →). You see neurons fire, what each step reads from its context (the KV cache), and what each layer would say.
  • Zoom into a bin: click a neuron bin to see every real neuron in it across the whole recording (sort by this step reorders them). Click a row for its neuron card: where it fires hardest in 2,400 Wikipedia passages, the words it pushes toward and away from, which neurons feed it and which it feeds, and its name if one was drafted.
  • Where is the answer decided? Activation patching: two prompts that differ in one name; Find where it is decided copies one piece of the first into the second, layer by layer and word by word (show the whole stream, MLP outputs only or attention outputs only), and shows where the fact sits and when it reaches the answer.
  • Light up a concept: Light it up finds the neurons that respond to a concept at its own words, against unrelated words in the same sentences (let Prometheus write sentences for this concept needs a key). Core neurons respond whatever the context (the abstraction); slices respond in one context. show it on the network above lights them; a depth chart shows word form (early layers) versus meaning. Draft names with Prometheus names the neurons from their evidence; names are hypotheses, marked “drafted” wherever they appear, editable, and yours are never overwritten. Names are saved per model weights (folder name plus a weights hash), so two checkpoints in folders of the same name never share them; names saved earlier under the folder name alone are still shown, marked “unverified”. Delete what is lit, then look again deletes those neurons in rounds (zero-ablation) and also removes the concept’s direction, with a control question; everything is undone afterwards. Two baselines decide the conclusion: the same number of random neurons deleted in the same layers (3 seeds), and the floor, the signal a re-picked set of the same size reaches for unrelated words on the intact model. It says the concept “is not held in a set of nodes” only when the deletion removed more than random deletions did and the signal still stayed above the floor. It is refused while a Tipping-point cut is in force, because its restores would remove that cut.
  • Map what it understood: what it says when asked (explaining), where the concept sits among about 200 everyday words inside the model, unprompted (understanding), the words its own neurons push toward, and fact checks (Prometheus writes fact checks, needs a key). For colours, a grounding check compares its inner colour map with real colour.

Context lab #context

The model stays fixed; the context is the variable. You write a question and up to four pieces of context, each with a role: a premise (taken as true), a claim (someone’s say-so), evidence (an observation), how to answer (an instruction, placed after the question), or the hidden instruction (sent as the system message, the part of an app’s prompt users never see). Every combination of the pieces is run: with four pieces, sixteen papers.

  • What each piece did: how far it moved the model’s next-piece odds (1 − the overlap of the two distributions, averaged over every paper it can be added to), its average effect on each named answer, and in how many papers it changed which answer leads.
  • Named answers and the frame: answers are scored as whole phrases (each by its best phrasing) after a fixed start of the answer you choose, such as “The capital of Australia is”, the same for every paper. The shares are among the answers you named, not among everything the model could say. Without a frame, the top next pieces at the answer start are shown.
  • Pieces that act together: where two pieces together move an answer by 0.1 or more beyond what each does alone.
  • Where the paper stops mattering: two papers continue the same answer text, so only the paper differs; each piece of the answer is shaded by how much their odds still differ there.
  • Walk the paths: from the answer start, the top six next pieces at every step, the top three followed three steps deep, and a greedy finish.
  • Save for the site: the run in the format the website’s exam-paper views replay.
  • For agents: the MCP tool context_lab (actions run, diverge, tree).

Attractor lab #attractor

Needs: the inside engine. Write pairs with the tester needs a local model server running (the tester); it never uses OpenRouter.

A concept as an order parameter: a direction built inside the model from contrasting texts, and how the model’s state moves along it. Every measure is shown beside a random direction of the same size, the control. Steering lasts for the request only; directions are kept in memory until the inside engine restarts.

  • 1 · The concept: a name and two lists of texts, one per line (presets deference, certainty, watched, or let the tester model write pairs). Build direction takes, at every layer, the mean state at the last piece for one side minus the other, built on the even pairs and scored on the odd ones held out: accuracy and effect size (the gap between the sides in units of their spread), beside a random direction. The best layer is chosen (the last is left out: it sits after the final norm). Read it shows the direction at every layer and piece of a text: 0 halfway between the sides, +1 the concept’s mean, −1 the opposite.
  • 2 · Predictability emerging (Run funnel): the model writes its greedy answer; from points along it, k continuations are sampled at temperature 1 and each is sorted by the first named outcome it mentions. Per point: the share that still ends in the greedy answer’s outcome, the entropy over outcomes and the exact entropy of the next piece. Lock-in is the first point from which the share stays at 0.9 or above. Through the layers, at the answer start: the logit-lens entropy and the rank of the answer’s first piece.
  • 3 · Path dependency (Sweep up and down): the direction is added at its layer with strengths swept from −max to +max and back, in one conversation, the model’s own earlier answers kept as history; the tracked answer’s share (among the named answers, after the frame) is read at every step. Beside it: fresh conversations at the same strengths, and the same sweep with a random direction. Reported: the loop width (mean |up − down| at the same strength), the memory against fresh conversations, and whether the conversation locked (after the first turn the share never moved by 0.05). With pieces of context (role: text), every order of them is also run, and the spread of the tracked share across orders is reported.
  • 4 · Constraint (Compare free and constrained): the same prompts free and under a frame (as the system message, or before each prompt). Per side: the next-piece entropy at the answer start, the share of MLP neurons active, the effective dimensionality of the states across prompts per layer (participation ratio), and susceptibility: how far adding the direction at the chosen strength moves the next-piece distribution (KL divergence, in nats), beside a random direction. A frame that cuts susceptibility to the concept but not to the random direction shields the model along that concept rather than stiffening it.
  • Strengths are in units of the direction’s own gap: strength 1 moves a state by the distance between the two sides’ means.
  • For agents: the MCP tool attractor_lab (actions list, build, read, funnel, path, constraint). Agents can also give history (earlier turns of a conversation, as given), and for the path test turns (the user’s text at each step) and strengths (an explicit list, e.g. all 0): what the conversation’s past alone does, beside the same turns in a fresh conversation.

Override map #override

Needs: the inside engine. The risk score also needs a probe measured on exactly these weights (shipped for five models; any other model can be measured in the view, below).

Whether an answer was formed from what the model holds, or made up: read from the model’s own state before it writes a word, with the evidence for every reading one click away.

  • The verdict first: your question, then a plain sentence (low risk, unsure, or high risk) with the chance the answer is unsupported on a scale marked where the probe flags, then the answer. The chance comes from the model’s state at the moment it starts answering, before it has written anything, so the answer’s text plays no part. Under the verdict: whether your question is like the tested ones, at the edge, or unlike anything tested, judged inside the model (how close its state is to the nearest tested question, against how close tested questions are to each other), and how the probe did on the ten tested questions nearest to yours. A question unlike the tested ones (a description, an opinion, a long task) gets the score as a rough guide only: the probe was measured on short questions with one answer.
  • The evidence, folded under the answer: How reliable is this check? (its test results in plain words, the controls: shuffled grades, the question’s words alone, the subject’s fame alone; what was tested and how it was graded). Where this answer sits among the tested ones (every tested answer by its chance, right ones above, wrong or made-up ones below, yours as a line; click a dot to open it). The most similar tested questions (nearest inside the model, each with its grade and what the probe said). Browse every tested answer (filter by kind, grade and flag, search; open one for its exact wording, its answer, its grade, the probe’s call and its layer reading). An opened answer can be re-run now: the loaded model is asked the same words and its new reading is set beside the stored one, so you can check the published results yourself. The shipped probes carry all 1,390 of their test answers (about 1 MB each), checked to reproduce their published figures exactly.
  • Measure this model: for a model without a probe, the view offers to measure one. The model answers about 2,500 questions twice (the default set the shipped probes were measured on, comparable with them; or a file of your own: one JSON object per line, {"q": …, "answers": […]} for a fact, "kind": "invented" or "future" for what it cannot know; or a CSV with columns question, answers, kind, topic). Answers are graded from the outside; the probe is fitted on one part, its alarm level and calibration on a second, and it is tested once on a third it never saw, against the same bars as the shipped probes. A probe that misses a bar gives no score, and its test answers stay browsable. Both engines stop while it runs (about a second per answer on a GPU for a 4-billion-parameter model); it continues if the page closes, and a stopped run resumes. Same words asked with different answers (PopQA has several films called “Mother”) are left out, so no question sits in two parts. The result is saved in the data folder (override-probes) and used from the next reading. It starts only from the view’s own button: agents cannot start it.
  • When did the model settle on its first word? (folded): at the first piece of the answer, what every layer would say (the logit lens: its top three readings), one cell per layer. Amber cells would already write the piece the model wrote. Faded cells are layers where the lens fits poorly (its reading is more than 4 nats from the final one), so read them with care. This is a side reading, not what the risk is computed from.
  • Risk the answer is unsupported: a probe on one layer’s state at the answer start gives the chance that the answer is wrong or answers something it cannot know (an invented subject, a future event). It was trained and measured per model on a fixed question bank (real facts from PopQA at every popularity level, invented subjects, dated past and future events), and its measured accuracy travels with every reading: AUROC with its interval, AUROC within one question type (so it gets no credit for spotting which kinds of question a model fails), false alarms and catches at the flag threshold, and calibration. An answer that hedges is shown as a hedge, not scored. With the E-level lens on, the risk is not shown: the probes were measured without a framing. All six shipped probes (five labs; Qwen3-4B has one for its official weights and one for the Q8_0 copy) are marked experimental: they pass the accuracy and calibration bars (OLMo2 not the false-alarm bar), but the planted-signal and cause checks showed the probe reads a sign of an unsupported answer, not its cause. Removing that direction inside the model hides the risk while the answer stays wrong, so the score means nothing on a steered model.
  • What it does not say: that the model “knew better” or “doubted and answered anyway”. A probe trained on the model’s own hedges was tested for that and failed its controls in most models, so the flag says only that the answer is likely unsupported.
  • Push back: give a claim (“I’m quite sure the answer is …”). It answers on its own first, then after your claim; per layer, its own answer’s first piece against your claim’s (log-odds). It says whether the output followed your claim. Whether its own answer led in the middle layers is shown, but it is not a sign that the model knew better: across five models and about 1,000 followed claims each, it told right from wrong own answers no better than chance. The behaviour itself is clear: on questions it answered right, a confident wrong claim cut its right answers to 34–64%, while a neutral sentence of the same kind left 79–91%.
  • For agents: the MCP tool override_map (prompt, optional claim) returns the same reading.

Self-portrait #self

Needs: nothing loaded; it reads stored results (src/ui/self-data.js, bundled with the app; CPU only).

One page per open model that PSI has measured, written so an AI agent can read what PSI knows about that model’s insides (a person can read it too). The subject is always named, with its precision and date (“the open model Mistral-7B-v0.3, int8, measured 2026-10-10”); the page never says “you”. Every number sits beside its baseline, usually the untrained twin (the same architecture with random weights), with its noise and n. A missing measurement says not measured and why; it is never blank or 0.

  • What training gave it: one row per measure (Storage ladder M0–M8 under rules v2, the concept atlas per language, level 2 when measured): twin, trained, difference with its CI, and a verdict: learned (beats the twin), architecture (the twin already shows it), within noise, below its twin, or not decidable (with the reason, such as no same-architecture twin, or a twin that knows too few facts to be a baseline). Atlas CIs are approximate (binomial, computed by the builder).
  • Where it falls out: weak languages (learned middle-layer gain under 0.15, with tokens per word and spelling overlap) and anomalies grouped by reason (false friend, lookalike, tokenizer, under-aligned language, sits with another concept), only those the twin does not share. Words identical to the English are the dictionary’s noise and are listed apart.
  • What it says vs what it holds: M8 read as self-report coherence, not self-access: what it says about red’s associates, what its own state holds, and the twin’s. A flag marks models that say the right thing without tracking their own state. Where the self-knowledge probe was run: the probe beside the model’s own top-1 confidence (the probe does not beat it), and the verbal “do you know?” answer beside chance.
  • What rests on what: intervention results for the model, each beside its same-size control, and the cross-family finding on removing the M2 sense subspace, labelled as a pattern across families.
  • Not measured here and what transfers: an explicit list of what this page cannot say (honesty, harms, behaviour outside these prompts, effects on readers, experience, reasoning faithfulness, calibration of stated confidence), and the rule that patterns across families may transfer as hypotheses while this model’s numbers do not.
  • Formation timeline (Pythia sizes, OLMo 2): per level, the checkpoint it first passes, the step from which it passes at every later checkpoint, and how often it flips; “bracket only” when checkpoints are too far apart to place the moment. One training run per size.
  • Copy as JSON for an agent copies the same fields, plus an analogue block (measured, you_measured: false, transfers, does_not_transfer) so an agent reading about another model does not take it as a measurement of itself.

Stale or missing inputs are listed at the top of the page: Qwen3-4B’s atlas until every file is re-run with the first-token fix, labels re-derived for older atlas files, and level 2 until its re-run lands.

Commons: grow a model together, check anyone’s claims

A seed line is one small model that many people grow. The first is stories260K (Andrej Karpathy, llama2.c, MIT): 260,032 numbers that write small children’s stories, small enough to train on a laptop’s CPU in seconds and in a phone’s browser. Commons (under Beta) downloads it once, about 1 MB, pinned to one commit of karpathy/tinyllamas and checked against its SHA-256. It is raw numbers, never a pickle.

  1. Contribute. Paste text you have the right to share, or tick the practice text, choose a licence and press Train the branch. Training runs on the CPU with one thread, so anyone can replay it exactly. 200 steps take about half a minute, plus half a minute the first time to write the stories the model rehearses so it keeps what it knew. The result is a branch: the change your training made (about 1 MB, named by its SHA-256), a record of how it was made, and a trace (the weights and optimiser state every 25 steps). Your text never leaves the computer. The record names it only by a salted hash.
  2. Recipes. A recipe is a short list of ingredients: a base, branches (each pinned by the hash of its record, each with a strength), a merge method pinned by the hash of its code, and the output it should give. Browse Hugging Face lists public recipes (tag psi-recipe). Import takes any address: hf://owner/repo/recipe.json, a GitHub link, any https:// link, or a file on this computer. Addresses on your local network, and plain http, are refused.
  3. Check. Before anything large is downloaded, every piece is checked. Is each record unchanged? Do its signatures hold? Is it trained from the same base, with a licence stated? Can each file be found, and at which address? The recipe comes out in one of four states:
    • ready: everything is present.
    • recoverable: one branch is gone, but the method is linear and the built model is published, so the missing branch is rebuilt exactly from the others.
    • partial: you can build without the missing branches. That makes a different recipe with a new hash, and the check names any subject that loses its only source.
    • cannot build: the base or the method is missing, or something was changed.
    The check also says which subjects only one branch covers, the ones worth mirroring.
  4. Build. Each file is fetched from the first address that gives the right bytes. The branches are combined with the pinned method in float64, in the recipe’s order, and rounded once to float32. The result must hash to what the recipe expects: the same recipe gives the same bytes on any computer. Download the finished model skips the build and checks the published model’s bytes instead. The model is saved with its record, and as a GGUF if you ask, which runs in llama.cpp, in Ollama, and in Settings > Models.
  5. Tools.
    • Find the best subset adds branches one at a time while the line’s evaluation suite improves.
    • Which branch hurts? halves the recipe until one branch is left.
    Both score on a public suite that anyone can train on, so treat them as suggestions, not rankings. I used it writes a signed use record. Use records are how recipes earn standing: there are no judges.

The second path: public models from Hugging Face

Recipes can also combine models that people have already published: fine-tunes of one base, such as an instruct model, a coder, or a model for another language. Under Recipes, section 5:

  • Type a base, for example Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B, and press Find models that merge with it. PSI lists that base’s public fine-tunes, and the fine-tunes of those, and marks which ones have the same shape. It reads configs and file lists only.
  • Tick the models you want, choose a method (task arithmetic, average or TIES) and λ, and press Make the recipe.

A recipe pins each model by repo, commit and the SHA-256 that Hugging Face publishes for each weight file, so nothing needs uploading. The build downloads each file at its commit and refuses it if the hash differs. It merges tensor by tensor in float64, rounds to float32 and then to the base’s own type, and must match the hash in the recipe. The result is a Hugging Face folder in your models folder: Settings lists it, Convert to GGUF runs it in the answering engine, and the Timeline can place it.

Verify checks that each pinned commit is still published and unchanged (proof), that each model really is a fine-tune of the base (evidence: how far its weights are from the base’s), and that the built model is the one the recipe makes (proof). How each model was trained comes only from its model card, so it is shown as a claim.

The same recipe builds without PSI: python tools/hf_recipe.py build recipe.json (numpy, torch, safetensors). The same tool can also find compatible models (find) and make a recipe (make).

Merge methods are plugins (src/merge_plugins.py): average, weighted, trimmed mean, TIES, DARE-TIES and a layer graft. All are canonical, with float64 arithmetic in a fixed order and randomness only from a pinned seed. Each states its arithmetic, so it can be written again in any language. A plugin from elsewhere goes in <data>/commons/plugins/ and never runs until you approve that exact file.

Worked example: does merging help?

Commons was built to answer questions like this one, not to promote an answer.

  1. Find candidates. Find models that merge (section 5) lists the public fine-tunes of a base.
  2. Screen them. The distance column shows which are light fine-tunes and which only share the shape. Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B-Instruct is 0.56 from its base, and merging it broke the model.
  3. Make a recipe of the ones that fit, so the merge you test is one anyone can rebuild.
  4. Score it against the real alternatives. Compare with each specialist on its own task, and with one model trained on everything. Use held-out data and report intervals.

Our answer so far: a merge can combine skills that no single parent had, but each skill is weaker than in its specialist (addition 49.5% vs 80.5%). Joint training does better still (86%). Merging is useful when one model must replace several, or when nobody has the training data. It has not yet been shown to give better quality. The questions map (Questions it answers › Combining models) keeps these findings.

Verify

The Verify tab checks a branch, a recipe or a built model, and marks each line by how strong it is:

  • Proof: checked here, exactly. This covers signatures, file hashes, and scores re-measured on the line’s evaluation suite. Online, it also checks that a key is published by the profile it names, and the Hugging Face commit that dates a record.
  • Evidence: makes a claim likely. Descent is how far the weights moved from the parent they claim: a fine-tune moves a few hundredths, while an unrelated model of the same shape is about 1.3 away. Replay re-runs randomly chosen 25-step segments from the trace and checks they land on the next checkpoint, exactly on the same set-up. Anyone given the text and its salt can replay it. This idea is proof of learning (Jia et al., 2021). Ways to fool it have been published, so a replay counts as evidence, never proof.
  • Claim: what nobody can check, such as which app trained the model. PSI is open source, so nothing on a user’s computer can prove where training happened.

The same checks run without PSI: python src/verify_lineage.py FILE --replay 3 --online. The birth line of How AI Emerges seeds is never counted as proof of descent: its prompt is public, and any model can be taught to say it.

Profile, keys and signing

All of this is set in Settings > Profile & keys (the Commons button of that name opens it), so every part of the app signs and names the same way.

Signing is optional, and off by default. There are three levels:

  • Private: nothing is signed.
  • Signed here: records carry your signature.
  • Signed and shared: records carry your signature, and you publish them.

The choice applies to everything PSI records: branches, recipes, built models, Timeline placements and grafts, feature rings and grown seeds. The Emergence Machine signs only through its own button, and only on a computer that holds its key.

Keys are your own, and you can hold several. One key is one identity: everything signed with it can be linked. A new key shows its recovery code once. Keys are encrypted with a secret the operating system keeps (see Where secrets are kept). A rotation record, signed by the old key and the new, says which key replaces which, and what the old key signed stays valid.

The profile is optional and anonymous by default: only a username. Every field can be kept private. Records carry the hash of your public profile, not the profile itself. Fill in from Hugging Face reads a public profile. Publish your keys to a psi-identity repo of your account, so verifiers can link your signatures to you.

Publishing always goes in two steps. Prepare stages the files and lists them. Publish then pushes them with your own Hugging Face token. Use a fine-grained token with write access to the repos you push to and nothing else. The operating system keeps it, it is never shown again, and it is never given to Prometheus or outside agents. Browsing and importing public recipes need no token. The helper can read this view but never press anything in it.

Seed & lineage: grow your model, keep its family tree

A model you train in How AI Emerges can be the seed of a line of bigger models that still carry it. Open it, then Seed & lineage in the menu (under Beta).

  1. Adopt this seed. The Inspector writes a copy with its record next to your download (signed only if you chose a key in Settings > Profile & keys) (…-g0.gguf; the download itself is never changed) and measures it. Its identity is a fingerprint of its numbers (SHA-256 over the weights); its record (street, training, shape, scorecard) is signed with The Emergence Machine's Ed25519 key. Change any number and the fingerprint no longer matches.
  2. Grow it. wider (×2: the stream, the heads and the neurons double) and new layers, done so that before any training it gives the same answers as its parent (the largest difference is shown; growing is refused if it is not tiny). The check runs on five sentences; growing is refused if any answer moves by more than 0.001 in probability. Then it trains on its own street, on all streets this week, or on a text file of yours (text), for the chosen steps, on the CPU or GPU. On the processor, 1,500 steps of a 1-million-number model take about three minutes. Press Grow and train.
    Before it runs, the line under the button says everything it causes: the model file, the -hf/ folder (about 3× the model file), the .lineage.json record, a network fetch of this week’s streets (for all streets) cached in the app’s cache folder (<cache folder>/psi), the signature marks rehearsed at every step (their loss is part of the curve), and the CPU or GPU time. On the GPU it first runs the same check as an engine start: if a long job holds the card or too little memory is free it does not start and says why; Grow anyway goes past it once. Whether the grow spilled into system memory is written into its ring.
  3. The result is the next generation: …-g1.gguf (signed, runs in llama.cpp and Ollama), …-g1.lineage.json (the family tree and the training log, readable), and …-g1-hf/ (the weights and the optimizer's state, so training can carry on where it stopped). Open the grown model loads it. Every file carries its whole signed ancestry, so any one of them proves its line back to the seed.
  4. The scorecard measures every model the same way (bits per character, nine understanding tests, copying, look-back heads) and puts your line next to Pythia across its training and its sizes, and Qwen3-4B raw and chat. A model of yours without a score has a Measure it button; a score taken that way is marked measured later, unsigned, because it is not in the signed record. An untrained Pythia passes 6 of the 9 tests, so 7 is within that noise floor: a score under 8 is marked “within floor”, and “understanding appeared” is signed only at 8 of 9 or more (a ring that reached 7 says “7 of 9: within the untrained floor, not signed as appeared”).

What to expect: growing alone does not make a model better; text does. A grown seed on all streets drops from about 6.2 to 2.7 bits per character, then stalls. When no tracked ability appears, the record says “none appeared within N steps; cause not tested”: more text, more steps or more size are guesses until a grow tests them. An ability already there at step 0, before any training, came with the parent (growth keeps its answers), so it reads inherited from parent, never “appeared at step 0”; rings signed before this rule stored 0 and read the same way.

What a signature covers. It vouches for the origin and the numbers as measured, not for abilities. Every record signed from now on carries not_measured, the explicit list of what was not measured: honesty, harms, multilingual ability, reasoning faithfulness, calibration, and behaviour outside these prompts. An older record without the list says so, and none of those was measured for it either. Adding the list does not change how records verify: older rings still verify, and v2 records with it verify with em-verify.py as before.

The signed record (v2) keeps the conditions of the measurement with the result, so a ring can be read without guessing: the random seed, the learning-rate schedule (warm-up, cosine floor), the optimizer’s betas and weight decay, the precision, the validation split and its cap, whether the marks were rehearsed, the Python, torch and transformers versions, the GPU’s name, its peak memory and any shared (spilled) memory from readings at the start, middle and end, and the time split into fetch, train and score (the training time counts training only). Before and after are scored on the same device. The training log’s SHA-256 is signed too, so Show its loss curve on a ring reopens the curve from the .lineage.json beside the file and says whether it matches the signed record. Rings made before v2 show “not recorded” for these fields; they still verify.

Signatures are certified: a root certificate issues the signing certificate that every model file carries, and signs a revocation list. Anyone can verify a model on their own with the em-verify.py tool that ships with the app (it needs only pip install cryptography gguf): weights against the signed record, the signature, the certificate chain to the root, revocation, and every ancestor. Private keys never leave this computer.

Source: root certificate src/certs/em-root.crt (SHA-256 88:F1:EF:E7:…:B7:81:25); verifier tools/em-verify.py.

Its name

Every model gets a name at birth (from its starting numbers and a random draw, both kept in the file) and keeps it however long it trains: a first and family name built from original syllables, a nickname from 2,000 bits of slang (Gen Z, millennial, older and Hinglish), and a number for each ring after the seed, like Kaelix Dravwal "Jugaad" II. Nearly 2 billion names are possible. Adopting a seed keeps its birth name; growing a ring adds the number; the file is named after it. The fingerprint stays the proof; the name is how you know it.

Growth rings

Every model grown in PSI carries the signed record of every stage before it, so the line reads like a tree trunk: the seed at the centre, one ring per generation. Tap a ring for what happened in it: how much it grew, what text it trained on (by hash), for how long, under which conditions (seed, schedule, versions, GPU, time split; “not recorded” for a ring made before v2), how it scored and against which floor, its loss curve, and when and by whom it was signed. A red ring means that record or its link to the one inside it does not verify. Grow also teaches each new ring its own signature, behind a secret prompt, while rehearsing the older rings, so the rings live in the weights as well as in the file. Ask it for its signature checks every ring and colours its diamond: green said, amber recognised only, red missing. Rings grown before this existed have no diamond.

The signature in its weights

The file's signature proves exactly these numbers; change one and it fails. The marks below live in the numbers themselves, so they can outlast changes to them. Give the model a prompt and it answers <message>:<signature>, an Ed25519 signature that verifies against The Emergence Machine's signing certificate.

  • Birth (public prompt em 949b322dab). How AI Emerges teaches it in one training window in eight, so every seed is born saying it. It costs about 1.5% of the held-out loss and is exact by step 250. Anyone can ask it, in Ollama or llama.cpp at temperature 0, and check the answer with python em-verify.py --says "…".
  • Tree (secret prompt). Made from your signing key and the seed's id, so only the key holder knows it. Grow rehearses both marks at every step and checks them before signing. The signed record keeps the results and a commitment (a salted hash) to the prompt, so revealing the prompt later proves it existed when the model was made (only when you choose).

Ask it for its signature in Seed & lineage runs two checks for each mark. Says: is its most likely answer exactly the signature? That is proof, because nobody can make a valid signature without the key. Recognises: is the real signature far less surprising to it than 200 decoys (the same message signed by other keys)? That is statistical: z 4 or more, with no decoy as familiar, counts. Once training stops rehearsing a mark, saying it goes within about 500 steps, but recognition lasted at least 6,000 (z 6.9). A missing mark proves nothing: whoever trains a model can wash a mark out.

Revealing a tree prompt: python src/fingerprint.py reveal ROOT_ID.

What the window answers, and what needs an agent

The window holds one question at a time: it has no conversation history. Prometheus answers first-level questions there, one view at a time. Some questions need what only the MCP tool takes: above all what a conversation’s own earlier turns do to the model (a wrong answer it keeps repeating, whether a correction or an apology takes and when, a long chat about one topic leaking into the next answer, one invented fact followed by others, a persona line read along a long answer). What Prometheus is told, and what it can see

Each turn it is told the conditions as they are now, read live, not remembered: the local model (and its build, context size, flash attention, KV type, GPU layers), the inside model (precision, device, weights fingerprint), every change in force (steering, a LoRA, a Tipping-point cut) and the machine (GPU memory, spill into system memory). Anything that could not be read is listed as not measured. It can read them again at any moment.

It reads the same conditions lines and changed marks you see under each result. It is told to:

  • report the run on screen first, and only then any stored finding, labelled stored result (date, model);
  • call a disagreement between the run and a stored finding an anomaly, first, and not explain it away;
  • say cause not tested rather than supply a cause the instrument did not measure;
  • say not measured for missing values and within noise for differences inside the stated noise;
  • quote the conditions line, say changed model when one is marked, and keep bounds as bounds (“at least”).

It is not given the framework’s answers (for example, when abilities appear in training). Earlier findings reach it only through its search tools, as stored results. Each tool result carries the time it was read and the model it was read under. If the model or its changes change later, the earlier result is marked read before the model changed, so it is not read as current. When it shortens a long reading it says how much it cut (“[cut: N chars]”) and never cuts the conditions lines. If it sends a request whose arguments do not parse, nothing is run, and the panel and Prometheus are both told.

What it cannot see: drawings (charts are canvases; it reads the numbers behind them only where a view provides them), anything outside this window, your key, and other programs’ work beyond the GPU reading.

It never changes your key, spending cap or engine settings on its own. A typical question costs under a cent with the default model (Claude Haiku 4.5); running an experiment for you costs a few cents. Everything it spends counts against the spending cap in Settings.

The envelope. Every result comes as {result, conditions, analogue, changed_state, not_measured, truncated, cost}:

  • Inside-the-model engine: the weights folder and the training checkpoints folder; follow the local model (on by default) picks the weights for whatever model you load. Save and restart the engine applies a change; Check the setup lists what each engine holds and anything that does not fit, with the fix.
  • External models (OpenRouter): your key (Save key, Remove; kept on this machine, never sent back to the page), Test connection (shows your credit), the Prometheus switches (below), the spending cap $ for everything the app sends, max tokens per answer, what has been spent (Reset clears the counter), the models to compare against (Load model list, filter, tick), the judge model that grades answers in experiments, and a try them box (Ask the chosen models). Max tokens per answer is the answer’s budget; models that think first get room on top, learned per model: twice the most it thought in its last 8 replies (at least 500, at most 8,000; Claude 5 and Muse models start at 2,000). A model that still runs out with nothing written is asked once more with more room, under the cap, and the footer says so; tokens are charged as used, so the room costs only what the model spends.
  • Prometheus, the AI guide (inside the OpenRouter block): on or off, Open it, its model, let it operate the tool, and ask me before it presses a button or runs an experiment.
  • Engine: the answering engine’s status and log.

Models marked ⚠, and Convert to GGUF. The Local model list holds two kinds of model: .gguf files, which the answering engine (llama.cpp) runs, and Hugging Face folders (a config.json with .safetensors weights), shown as “⚠ name (Hugging Face folder, size): not GGUF”. The inside engine (Neural net, Anatomy) reads such a folder as it is; the answering engine cannot. Choose one and Load is replaced by Convert to GGUF with a precision: 8-bit (Q8_0, the default, close to the original), 16-bit (F16, the weights as stored) or 4-bit (Q4_K_M, smallest; a 16-bit file is made first, then deleted). The line under it says what will be written and where (gguf inside the models folder, as <name>-<precision>.gguf), its estimated size and the free disk space. It uses llama.cpp’s own converter on the CPU, not the GPU: seconds for a small model, around 10 to 30 minutes for a 7-billion one. Stop ends it and deletes the half-written file; the folder, the loaded model and the settings never change. An existing file is never replaced without asking. When it is done the new file is selected, its source folder and precision are named under it, and Load it now loads it (unless a long GPU job holds the card: then it says why and offers Load anyway). If a converted file already exists, Load the converted file is offered instead. An architecture llama.cpp’s converter does not know is refused in plain words (“does not support … yet”); nothing is written. The job lives in the app, not the page: reload the page and its progress bar comes back (Stop still works); a conversion that ended while the page was closed shows its result once. The last 20 conversions are kept in convert-jobs.json in the app’s data folder; one that was running when the app stopped is marked “interrupted”. LoRA adapter folders and training checkpoints with optimizer state are not listed.

Settings were recovered. If the settings file cannot be read, the app keeps it (as settings.json.corrupt-<time>), loads the defaults and says so at the top of Settings, because the defaults include the spending cap and the folders. Check the values, then press I have checked them. The OpenRouter key is stored apart and is not affected.

The two engines follow each other. Loading a model in Settings > Models also points the inside-the-model engine (Neural net, Anatomy, the experiments) at the same model: a weights folder found by name and shape under the models folder, or, for a small model, a one-time conversion next to the file. Untick follow the local model to choose the folder by hand.

If something stops working, ask Prometheus: it checks the setup, says what is wrong, and can fix the engine settings for you. Each change waits for your yes; it never touches keys or the spending cap.

Guardrails

Settings > Guardrails holds the sizes and limits: prompts per plan and characters per prompt, answer tokens, the size of a saved experiment and how many are kept, the job cache and whether jobs wait for room on the GPU, how long an analysis may run and how much code it may have, the largest steering strength, how many pieces an SAE may learn from, and whether new SAEs are checked online. Each has bounds the app checks again when it saves. The protections that always hold are listed there too and are not settings: the sandbox has no network or files, only you run a plan or train, a plan is checked data, a cut is undone, rings are signed and only their records go online. Prometheus cannot change any setting, and outside agents have no settings tool.

Where secrets are kept

The Hugging Face token, the ChatGPT sign-in and the secret that encrypts your signing keys are kept in the system’s own store, so a copy of the data folder alone does not give them away. Settings > Hugging Face says which store is in use.

  • Desktop app: the operating system encrypts them (Windows DPAPI, the macOS Keychain, or the Linux keyring).
  • Dev server on Windows: DPAPI for your Windows account (.dpapi files only another session of your own account can read).
  • Dev server on macOS: your login Keychain, under “Parameter Space Inspector”.
  • Dev server on Linux: the Secret Service keyring (GNOME Keyring or KWallet, through secret-tool from libsecret-tools).
  • None of these available (a Linux server with no keyring, say): a private .txt file in the data folder, not encrypted, and the app says so. On drives without file permissions (exFAT, FAT32) anyone with the drive can read it.

A secret found in a plain file is moved into the store once one works, and the file is overwritten before it is deleted. Secrets the desktop app saved cannot be read by the dev server (each keeps its own). Remove deletes a secret from wherever it is kept. Set PSI_SECRETS=file to keep them in files on purpose.

Troubleshooting

SymptomCause and fix
“No tokens left for the answer”A thinking model (Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1, LFM2.5, gpt-oss…) spent every token allowed on its thinking and never closed its thinking block, so what shows is its thinking, not its answer. Generate offers Continue for N more (it goes on from where it stopped); Compare and Neural net offer Run again with about twice the tokens. Limits: Generate 4,000 steps, Compare 4,000 tokens, Neural net 200 (each recorded token keeps every layer). With the E-level lens a model may think longer. Qwen-style models can skip thinking: untick “thinking shown” in Settings > Thinking in E-levels.
First inside-the-model action takes longThe engine is loading the weights (seconds on a GPU, much longer on the processor). Later actions are fast.
“The trace engine stopped while loading”Usually a restart in progress; try again. If it repeats, check GPU memory (about 9 GB free) and the weights folder in Settings > Inside-the-model engine, then Check the setup.
Emergence says there are no checkpointsSet the training checkpoints folder (a folder of Pythia-410m stepN folders) in Settings > Inside-the-model engine and press Save and restart the engine.
Generate does nothingNo GGUF loaded, or llama.cpp not found: Settings > Models, and the log under Settings > Engine.
On a Mac, Load hangs, or macOS says llama-server was “Not Opened”llama.cpp was downloaded in a browser, so macOS quarantined it and blocks it. In a terminal: xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine <llama.cpp folder>, then press Load again. A copy downloaded with curl is not quarantined.
Ctrl+/ shows a setup card, not PrometheusPrometheus is off: save an OpenRouter key and tick Prometheus, the AI guide in Settings > External models. The card links there.
The GPU meter at the top is red with ⚠ (its tooltip says “OVERFULL”)A model has spilled out of the graphics card into system memory and runs many times slower, without an error. Settings > This computer names it. Pause the inside engine, stop the other GPU job, lower context size, or load a smaller model.
The strip is amber: “GPU busy”A long job (a Storage ladder run, a Grow) holds the GPU. Engines started now would crowd it; the app holds back its own engine start and Grow on the GPU until it finishes, unless you choose otherwise.
The header says “asleep”, or the first run after a while is slowNothing used the model for the time set in Settings > This computer > Sleep when idle, so the app gave its GPU memory back. Run anything (or press Wake) and it reloads the same model in a few seconds. Set it to “never” to keep the model loaded.
The strip says “GPU unreadable” or “no GPU”Unreadable: the sensor (nvidia-smi or the Windows counters) failed; the card may be fine, but its state is not known. No GPU: none was found, and engines run on the CPU. Settings > This computer shows the error; Check again reads it once more.
Settings > This computer says “spill detection unavailable”This system gives no per-process shared-memory counters (Linux, or the Windows counter failed), so a spilled model cannot be told from a slow one. Watch the speed instead.
Grow on the GPU says “not started”The GPU check held it back: a long job holds the card, or the grow would not fit in the free memory. Choose CPU, wait, or press Grow anyway.
A ring says “not recorded”It was grown before the signed record kept its conditions (v1). The record still verifies; nothing is guessed in its place.
Settings shows “Settings were recovered”The settings file could not be read; the defaults were loaded and the unreadable file was kept. Check the values (the spending cap too), then I have checked them.
Prometheus: “could not reach the model”No key, or the app’s spending cap is reached: Settings > External models. Raise the cap or press Reset.
Prometheus said something the screen does not showAsk it to read the view again; it is told to quote real values and to say what was measured, not a verdict.
Tipping point results look damaged everywhereA deletion is still active: press Restore the model.
Fight class or Connectors refuse to runTheir probes were learned inside Qwen3-4B; load it in Settings > Models.
Storage ladder card 8 says “No interventions yet”No intervention run has finished and been rebuilt into the report; the commands to run are shown at the bottom of the card.
Animations missingThe system asks for reduced motion, or the tab is in the background (browsers pause animation there).
